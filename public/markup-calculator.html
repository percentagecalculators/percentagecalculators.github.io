Figuring out what to charge for something you sell always comes down to one question: how much are you adding on top of what it cost you? A Markup Calculator takes your cost and your target markup percentage and instantly returns the exact price to charge. Whether you're setting a price for the first time or double-checking a client quote, it turns a handful of arithmetic into one confident number.

What Is Markup? The Formula a Markup Percentage Calculator Uses

Markup is the amount you add on top of your cost to reach a selling price, expressed against that cost — not against what the customer eventually pays. That distinction trips people up constantly, since profit margin measures the same dollar figure against revenue instead. A markup percentage calculator like this one just automates the arithmetic:

$$ \text{Markup \%} = \frac{\text{Selling Price} - \text{Cost}}{\text{Cost}} \times 100 $$

Rearrange it and you can solve for the selling price directly, by multiplying cost by one plus the markup expressed as a decimal:

$$ \text{Selling Price} = \text{Cost} \times (1 + \text{Markup \%} \div 100) $$

A $40 product with a 50% markup works out to $60 — a $20 profit on the sale.

How to Work It Out by Hand

  1. Find your cost — everything it took to produce or acquire the item.
  2. Decide what you'll charge, or test a few options with the calculator above.
  3. Subtract cost from that figure to get your profit.
  4. Divide by cost and multiply by 100 to calculate the percentage.
  5. Compare it against the typical markup for your industry.

Markup, Margin, and Gross Profit Aren't the Same Thing

Three related numbers get confused constantly: markup, margin, and gross profit. Gross profit is simply the dollar difference between cost and sale price — no percentage involved yet. Markup turns that dollar figure into a percentage of cost; margin turns it into a percentage of the sale instead, and ends up smaller as a result.

Some competitors call the underlying markup formula something else entirely, but the math to calculate it never changes: find your cost, find your revenue, and run the numbers. Markups vary wildly even within one catalog — a retailer might calculate a modest markup on staple products and a much steeper one on premium products. For example, two items with an identical cost price can carry very different markups once you factor in what each is actually worth to the customer.

Margin Calculator vs. Markup: What's the Real Difference?

Searching for this kind of tool sometimes turns up a similar-looking calculator that divides by revenue instead of cost. This markup vs margin mix-up is one of the most common mistakes small business owners make, and it quietly leads to underpricing whatever you sell.

Quick tip: a 50% markup is always a 33.3% margin, never the reverse — keep that in mind if you're quoting numbers to a lender or investor who expects one and not the other.

Which Number Should You Actually Use?

Neither is "more correct" — they answer different questions. Markup is usually the starting point, since you're building a number up from a known cost. Financial statements lean on margin instead, because it describes what share of revenue actually became profit. In finance, sound accurate pricing judgment matters more than which formula you reach for first — a markup margin calculator or a price calculator is only as good as the cost data you feed it.

How Do I Convert Between Them?

Markup ÷ (100 + markup) × 100 gives you the equivalent margin, and margin ÷ (100 − margin) × 100 converts it back to markup. Either direction, a pricing calculator that only shows one of the two numbers is giving you half the picture.

What Your Markup Needs to Cover

A markup built only on the sticker cost of goods sold (COGS) will always fall short. Before any of it counts as real profit margin, it has to absorb:

  • Cost of goods sold — materials, manufacturing, or wholesale purchase price
  • Overhead costs — rent, utilities, software, and everything else that keeps the business running
  • Marketing costs and other sales costs — advertising, commissions, packaging
  • Transaction costs — payment processing fees, shipping, returns
  • Your desired profit — what you actually want to take home after all of the above

Add those up and compare the total against your gross margin, not just the headline markup number — a company with tight costs but weak control over overhead can still lose money on a paper-perfect markup.

How to Use a Free Markup Calculator to Set Your Selling Price

This tool takes three inputs and solves for whichever one you leave blank. Say you run a small business selling handmade candles with a product cost of $8 each. Enter that alongside a 60% markup and you get a selling price of $12.80 — no separate spreadsheet or profit calculator needed. Update the inputs the moment your product cost changes; acting on a stale number is one of the fastest ways to quietly lose money, and consistent numbers also make financial planning far easier down the line.

Cost-Plus Pricing and Other Strategies Beyond a Basic Markup Calc

The most common strategy built on markup is cost-plus pricing: total up every cost, add a fixed rate on top, and that's your number. It's simple and guarantees profitability on paper, but it ignores what competitors charge and what buyers will actually pay.

When a Flat Rate Breaks Down

This flat-rate approach holds up fine for stable, predictable products. It struggles in categories with volatile marginal cost, where producing one more unit gets cheaper or pricier with demand and seasonality — a flat markup rate can leave real money on the table either way.

Value-Based and Market-Based Alternatives

Value-based pricing sets the number around what a product is worth to the buyer rather than what it cost to make — software and consulting both lean on this. Reading the market instead starts from what the competition already charges and works backward from there. Both are worth layering on top of a mark up calculator once you have a baseline figure to react to.

Common Mistakes Sellers Make With a Mark Up Calculator

Even with a calculator doing the arithmetic, it's easy to land on the wrong number for reasons that have nothing to do with math. A few patterns show up again and again in real quotes and product sheets.

Rounding Too Early

Round your markup to a clean number before you calculate the final figure and small errors compound across a whole order. Carry the decimal through to the last step, then round once, at the end, when you present the number to a buyer.

Forgetting What's Bundled In

A quote that only accounts for the item itself and skips shipping, packaging, or a payment processor's fee will look profitable on paper and lose money the moment it ships. Build every real expense into the base figure before you apply a markup on top of it, not after.

Copying a Competitor's Number Blindly

Two sellers can charge the exact same amount and have wildly different outcomes if one runs a more efficient setup than the other. Matching a competitor's storefront figure without knowing their unit costs behind it is a guess dressed up as a strategy.

Treating Every Order the Same

A bulk order, a rush order, and a one-off custom request rarely deserve identical treatment. Many sellers quietly build in a lower figure for volume and a higher one for anything that demands extra time or a tighter deadline — the calculator above handles each scenario in seconds if you re-run it with fresh inputs.

None of these mistakes require a new formula to fix. Revisit the numbers on a schedule — monthly for anything with volatile inputs, quarterly for everything else — rather than setting a figure once and forgetting about it. A five-minute check-in with fresh inputs catches most of the problems above before a customer ever notices.

Markup Calc Examples You Can Put to Use

Getting comfortable with this kind of math early is an underrated entrepreneurship skill, and it compounds into real business growth over time. Calculate the same product's numbers with a price markup calculator across a few real scenarios and the logic starts to click:

Markup Examples Worth Reusing

  • A boutique buys a jacket wholesale for $60 and marks it up 100%, selling at $120 — a markup amount of $60 and a markup ratio of exactly 1:1 against cost.
  • A café's cost price on a coffee is $0.70; a 400% markup brings the menu figure to $3.50, typical where volume is low and margins have to work harder.
  • An e-commerce shop selling a $25 accessory runs a 20% seasonal markdown, dropping it to $20 without erasing the underlying markup entirely.
  • A consultant billing $150 an hour isn't working from an hourly cost at all — that's value-based pricing, where COGS barely enters the equation.
  • Tax or VAT added at checkout raises the customer's total further still, but neither belongs inside the markup math itself.

Whatever label you use — markup, margin, or profit percentage — the figure only means something if you're comparing it against the same baseline every time. Different markups get layered on at different stages of a sale, from a base sales tax to a broader pricing strategy, so keeping your price management consistent matters as much as the math itself. This is all part of ordinary sales strategy in accounting terms: price is usually the first thing a customer evaluates, and a business built on accurate, consistent numbers holds up better than one built on guesswork and a net loss waiting to happen.
