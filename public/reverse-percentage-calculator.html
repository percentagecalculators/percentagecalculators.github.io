Ever been handed a final price, a discounted total, or a raised wage and needed to work backward to the number it started from? A Reverse Percentage Calculator does exactly that — enter the final value and the percent applied, and it hands back the original value along with the math behind it. This reverse percentages calculator approach also works as an original value calculator for surcharges and grading problems, so whether you're checking a sale price or reconstructing a wage before a raise, you can skip the algebra and get the original number in seconds.

What Is a Reverse Percentage Calculator?

A regular percent question starts with the original number and asks what it becomes after a change. A reverse question flips that around: you already know the final value and the percent applied, and you need to find the original amount that produced it. This is sometimes called an inverse percentage problem, because you're solving backward from the result instead of forward from the start.

You'll run into this everywhere outside the classroom — a receipt that shows a discounted total but not the starting price, a payslip that shows a new wage but not the starting figure before the raise, or a textbook question that gives a score and asks for the full score it came from. Any time the wording is "X is P% of what number?" or "after a change, the result was X — what was it before?", you're looking at a reverse calculation.

Reverse Percentage Calculator vs a Forward Calculator

A forward calculator takes an original value and a percent, then multiplies to get a result — the calculation most people learn first. A reverse one does the opposite: it divides the final number by the adjusted percent to recover the starting figure. Spotting which direction a question asks is the first step, and it's the difference between multiplying and dividing when you solve by hand.

If the final amount came from an increase, the original will always be smaller than what you started with; if it came from a decrease, the original will always be larger.

The Reverse Percentage Formula, Explained

Every reverse calculation on this page reduces to one of three equations, depending on whether the change was a decrease, an increase, or a plain proportion.

Percentage Decrease: Discounts and Markdowns

When a value has dropped — a discount, a markdown, a pay cut — divide that final value by (1 minus the rate):

$$ \text{Original} = \frac{\text{Final}}{1 - \frac{\text{Rate}}{100}} $$

Shoes priced at $80 after a 20% markdown had a starting price of $80 ÷ 0.80 = $100.

Percentage Increase: Surcharges and Markups

When a value has gone up — sales tax, a markup, a raise — divide it by (1 plus the rate):

$$ \text{Original} = \frac{\text{Final}}{1 + \frac{\text{Rate}}{100}} $$

A gadget priced at $240 after a 20% surcharge had a pre-surcharge value of $240 ÷ 1.20 = $200.

Percentage of a Number: The Simplest Case

When the question reads "P% of a number is Z," divide the part by the rate:

$$ \text{Original} = \frac{\text{Part}}{\frac{\text{Rate}}{100}} $$

If 35% of a number is 320, the original number is 320 ÷ 0.35 = 914.29.

Worked Examples: Reverse Percentage Calculations in Action

Seeing these equations applied to real numbers makes the pattern click faster than the algebra alone — see the worked example below for exactly how the numbers move.

Example 1 — Starting Price After a Markdown

You paid $262.50 for a jacket after a 20% markdown. Step 1) The remaining rate is 100% − 20% = 80%. Step 2) $262.50 ÷ 0.80 = $328.13. Step 3) The original price, before the markdown, was $328.13.

Example 2 — Pay Before a Raise

Your new pay is $54,000 after a 12% raise. Step 1) The adjusted percentage rate is 100% + 12% = 112%. Step 2) $54,000 ÷ 1.12 = $48,214.29. Step 3) Your base salary before the raise was about $48,214.29 — a plain example of an original value calculator at work.

Example 3 — Original Test Score

A student scored 45 points, which is 60% of the full score. Step 1) The rate is 60%, or 0.60. Step 2) 45 ÷ 0.60 = 75. Step 3) The full score was 75 points.

Example 4 — Starting Value of an Account

An account is worth $12,600 after 8% growth. Step 1) The adjusted rate is 108%. Step 2) $12,600 ÷ 1.08 = $11,666.67. Step 3) The starting value of the account was $11,666.67, before that period of change began.

Reverse Percentage Increase vs Decrease: Getting the Direction Right

The single most common mistake in this kind of math is using the wrong sign — adding when you should subtract, or the reverse. Before you calculate, ask whether the result must be bigger or smaller than what it started as.

Original Price Finder: Confirming a Decrease

If the result came from a drop — a markdown, a pay cut — the original will always be larger than what you started with. Use the (1 − rate) form, never plain subtraction of the percent from the total.

Base Value Calculator: Confirming an Increase

If the result came from a rise — a surcharge, a raise — the original will always be smaller than what you started with. Use the (1 + rate) form instead.

Common Mistakes When Reversing a Percentage Change

  • Subtracting instead of dividing — a 20% discount does not mean subtracting 20 from the total; it means dividing by 0.80.
  • Forgetting to convert the rate to a decimal — 20% must become 0.20 before it goes into the equation.
  • Confusing percentage change with percentage points — a jump from 20% to 25% is a 5 percentage point increase, not a 5 percent increase, and treating the two as a percentage difference will throw off the whole answer.
  • Mixing up increase and decrease equations — double-check whether the scenario is a markup or a markdown before you divide, and re-check your work if the result seems off enough to suggest a percentage error.

Reverse Percentage Calculators for Compound, Multi-Step Changes

A single calculator like this one handles one change at a time. If a value went through more than one change in sequence — a markdown followed by a surcharge, or two years of compound percentage growth — you need to undo each step individually, working from the final result back to the very first number, one rate at a time.

Tip: when reversing a multi-step change, always undo the most recent adjustment first, then work backward through each earlier step in order — this is the same logic banks and accountants use for backward analysis of historical figures.

Manual Method: Division and Multiplication, No Calculator Needed

You don't need a calculator to solve a reverse percentage by hand — the process follows the same three steps every time. Step 1) turn the rate into a fraction. Step 2) divide the final number by that fraction to find what 1% (or 1 unit of the ratio) equals. Step 3) multiply by 100 to reach the full original amount. This manual method is worth learning once, even if you use the calculator afterward, because it's the same formula behind every example above.

Tips for Accurate Results

  • Round only at the final step — rounding a decimal too early can shift the original number by a noticeable amount.
  • Keep the rate as a fraction during the calculation, and only convert back to a percent or currency at the end.
  • Re-read the question to confirm whether it describes an increase, a decrease, or a plain ratio before you pick an equation.

Whichever scenario you're solving — a markdown, a surcharge, a raise, or a plain percentage-of-a-number question — this reverse percentage calculator gives you the original value instantly, with every step shown so you can verify the math yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q.How do I find the original price before discount?
Divide the sale price by (1 - discount%). If you paid $75 after 25% off: $75 ÷ 0.75 = $100 original.
Q.If $30 is 20% of a number, what is the number?
The number is 150. Calculation: ($30 ÷ 20) × 100 = $150.
Q.How do I work backwards from a percentage increase?
If current value is after X% increase, divide by (1 + X/100). Value of 115 after 15% increase: 115 ÷ 1.15 = 100.
Q.What was the price before 30% off if I paid $70?
Original was $100. Since you paid 70% of original: $70 ÷ 0.70 = $100.
Q.If 15 is 25% of what number?
15 is 25% of 60. Calculation: (15 ÷ 25) × 100 = 60.
Q.How do I find 100% from a partial percentage?
Divide the known value by the percentage (as decimal), or use: (Value ÷ Percentage) × 100.
Q.What is the formula for reverse percentage?
Original = (Part ÷ Percentage) × 100, or equivalently: Part ÷ (Percentage/100).
Q.If $48 is 60% of the original, what was the original?
Original was $80. Calculation: ($48 ÷ 60) × 100 = $80.
Q.How do I reverse a percentage decrease?
If current is X after Y% decrease, current = (100-Y)% of original. Original = current ÷ ((100-Y)/100).
Q.What number is 45 if that's 90%?
45 is 90% of 50. Calculation: (45 ÷ 90) × 100 = 50.
Q.How do I find pre-tax amount from tax paid?
If tax amount is known and rate is R%: Pre-tax = (Tax Amount ÷ R) × 100.
Q.If 75% of a number is 150, what is 100%?
100% is 200. Calculation: (150 ÷ 75) × 100 = 200.
