Getting a number from your boss is one thing — knowing what it actually means for your paycheck is another. This Salary Increase Calculator takes your current salary and your raise percentage or flat raise value, then hands back your new salary and the exact increase amount for any pay schedule. It's also called a pay raise calculator, pay rise calculator, or pay rise amount calculator depending on where you live, and whether you're heading into a salary negotiation, weighing a job offer, or just planning the next stage of your career, the math below shows exactly how those figures are built.

How This Pay Raise Calculator Turns Your Raise Into a New Salary

Whether you want to calculate a salary increase for a single raise or double-check an employer's math, this tool only needs three inputs to work: your current salary, how often you're paid, and the raise itself. Enter a percentage (say, a 5% raise) or a flat raise value, and it converts everything for you automatically. Doing that math by hand across every pay schedule gets tedious fast — that's the entire reason this exists. The sections below walk through each step, then show the same math manually so you understand what's happening underneath.

Enter Your Current Salary and Pay Period

Start with your current salary before tax or deductions, and pick the pay period that matches your paycheck. A standard 40-hour workweek is assumed by default, and the calculator will convert your figure into an hourly rate or a bi-weekly paycheck automatically if that's what you need.

Choose Your Raise Type and Raise Value

Next, pick the raise type: a percentage increase, a flat raise value, or simply the new pay rate you were quoted. Most raises are expressed as a percentage — a 3% cost-of-living adjustment, a merit-based bump — but if your employer already told you the new number, the calculator can work backward to find the percentage for you.

Read Your New Salary and Increase Amount

Once you submit those values, you get two outputs: your new salary and the increase amount in dollars. Both figures are shown gross, before tax, national insurance, or other deductions from your paycheck — useful for comparing offers, but remember your actual take-home pay will be somewhat lower. If you want the after-tax figure, run that gross number through a gross to net calculator next.

Salary Increase Percentage Calculator: The Pay Raise Percentage Formula, Step by Step

This salary increase calculator formula is exactly what's running under the hood. To find your new salary from a known raise percentage:

$$ \text{New Salary} = \text{Current Salary} \times \left(1 + \frac{\text{Raise \%}}{100}\right) $$

In plain steps: convert the percentage into decimal form (5% becomes 0.05), add 1 to get a multiplier, then multiply your old salary by that number. How to reverse the calculation: if you know both salary figures and need the percentage increase instead, subtract your starting salary from the new one, divide by the starting salary, then multiply by 100: \( \frac{\text{New} - \text{Old}}{\text{Old}} \times 100 \). Going from $45,000 to $48,600 is an 8% percentage increase.

Rounding matters more than it seems here. Carrying the full percentage through the calculation and rounding only the final dollar figure keeps things accurate; rounding the percentage itself first, then multiplying, can drift by several dollars. For anything you're actually going to rely on — a signed offer letter, a budget spreadsheet, a mortgage pre-approval — do the rounding last, not first.

Worked Example: A 7% Raise on a $52,000 Salary

Say your current salary is $52,000 and you're offered a 7% raise. Multiply $52,000 by 0.07 to get $3,640 — that's your increase amount. Add that to your old salary and your new salary is $55,640, or about an extra $303 in your monthly salary before tax.

Annual Salary vs. Hourly Pay

Because the annual salary figure is what most job offers quote, it's the easiest baseline for comparing raises between employers. But if you're paid hourly, converting that increase into an hourly figure is what actually shows up in your paycheck — a smaller-looking number, even though the percentage is identical.

Quick Salary Increase Calculator Answers for 2%, 5%, and 10% Raises

Some raise percentages come up so often it's worth memorizing the shortcut. For a 2% raise, multiply your salary by 0.02 and add the result — a $40,000 salary becomes $40,800. For a 5% raise, multiply by 0.05 — that same $40,000 becomes $42,000. For a 10% raise, just move the decimal point one place and add it — $40,000 plus $4,000 is $44,000. Think of it as a percentage calculator for salary changes: two numbers in, every pay schedule's new figure out. Unsure what pay raise percentage to expect? Cost-of-living adjustments typically land between 2% and 4% annually, and a performance-based raise usually runs 3% to 5%. These shortcuts work the same whether your figures are in dollars, pounds, or euros — the arithmetic doesn't change, only the currency symbol does. If you're comparing offers from employers in two different countries, convert both figures into one currency first and then run the calculation, rather than working in each currency separately and converting the results afterward.

When Employers Actually Give You a Raise

Raises aren't handed out at random — they tend to follow a handful of predictable triggers:

  • Length of service — many employers give raises annually simply for staying with the company.
  • Performance — tied to your annual review, and often the largest lever an employee controls directly.
  • Merit — new skills, added responsibilities, or a step up without a formal title change.
  • Inflation — if the cost of living rises faster than your wage, your employer may adjust your income to keep pace.
  • Law — a change to the minimum wage can force a raise regardless of performance.

How to Ask for a Bigger Raise

If your number isn't where you want it, plug a few target percentages into the calculator before your salary negotiation so you know exactly what to ask for. Come prepared with three things:

  • Evidence of your recent contributions or results, not just tenure.
  • A specific figure or range, not a vague request for "more."
  • A sense of what similar roles pay elsewhere, so your ask is grounded.

Even a well-argued case doesn't always land the full amount — but knowing your target number in advance means you'll recognize a fair counter-offer the moment you hear one.

Promotion Raises vs. Annual Increases

A promotion raise and a routine annual increase are calculated the same way, but they behave differently: an annual increase is usually a small, predictable bump, while a promotion resets your baseline entirely because it reflects a new role, not just a cost adjustment. If you want to see how repeated raises compound over several years, that's a job for a dedicated salary inflation calculator or a future salary calculator — this tool is built for the single-raise question, not the multi-year projection.

Why the Numbers Matter for Your Financial Future

A bigger number on a payslip is satisfying, but the real value of a raise is what it does to your personal finance position. Research on income and well-being suggests that happiness tracks closely with earnings only up to a point — after that, additional income matters less emotionally than it does practically, for budgeting, debt payoff, and long-term financial planning. That's exactly why knowing your precise new salary, rather than a vague sense that "it went up," matters for your financial future. Whether you're mapping out the next stage of your career, double-checking your payroll numbers, comparing wages against a competing offer, or estimating what taxes will take out of that new figure, deciding how much compensation is fair starts with the exact dollar amount this calculator gives you — not a guess. A clear budget update, a bigger retirement contribution, or simply peace of mind about your next loan application all start with that same figure.

This pay raise calculator is meant to give you that figure in seconds, not replace personalized financial or tax advice — for anything beyond a quick estimate, a qualified adviser or accountant can help you plan around it, especially once other factors like bonuses, benefits, or equity enter the picture.
