Dropping from one number to a smaller one happens everywhere — a sale price, a pay cut, or a town losing residents — and the percentage decrease calculator above turns that drop into a single, comparable figure. Enter your two numbers and it does the subtraction and division for you; below, you'll find the exact formula it uses, the reasoning behind each step, and enough worked examples that you could just as easily run the math by hand.

How to Calculate Percentage Decrease

The question is always the same: how big is the drop, relative to where you started? Follow these steps, whether you're comparing prices, salaries, or headcounts:

  1. Subtract the number you ended with from the number you began with, to find the raw difference.
  2. Divide that difference by the absolute value of the number you began with.
  3. Multiply the result by 100 to convert the decimal into a percentage.

If that last number comes out negative, the value actually went up, not down — a negative result is really a percentage increase in disguise, and it's worth double-checking which number you plugged in first.

Percentage Decrease Formula: Starting Value and Final Value

The formula behind the calculator is short enough to memorize and general enough to cover markdowns, pay cuts, and shrinking numbers alike:

$$ \text{Percentage Decrease} = \frac{S - F}{|S|} \times 100 $$

where S is the starting value and F is the final value — using \( \%\ \text{decrease} = 100 \times \dfrac{(S - F)}{|S|} \), you get the same result either way. The absolute value bars matter here: they keep the denominator positive even when the starting value itself is negative, which is why the sign of the answer always tells you increase or decrease, never the sign of the inputs.

Original Value vs. New Value in the Formula

It's easy to mix up which number goes where. The original value — also called the initial value — is always whatever you started with before the change happened; the new value is whatever you ended up at afterward. Swap them and the formula still runs, but you'll get a negative percentage instead of a decrease, since you'd effectively be measuring the increase back to where you started.

Tip: if your result reads as an increase when you expected a decrease (or vice versa), you've likely swapped which number came first — re-check which one represents the starting point.

Percentage Decrease vs. Percentage Increase

Percentage decrease and percentage increase share the same underlying formula; only the direction of the change differs. A percentage change calculation — sometimes labeled a percent change calculator — is the umbrella term for either direction, and a related but distinct tool, the percentage difference calculator, compares two values without assuming either one came first in time; it divides by the average of the two numbers rather than the starting one, so it won't always agree with a percentage decrease on the same pair of values.

Real-Life Applications of a Percentage Decrease Calculator

The math is simple, but where it gets used is what makes a percentage decrease calculator genuinely useful day to day — everyday mathematics as much as personal finance or basic economics.

Discount Pricing on an Original Price

Retail is the most common use case. If an item's original price is $80 and a 25 percent off coupon during a weekend sale drops the cost to $60, that's a straightforward 25 percentage reduction — the discount rate and the percentage decrease are the same number whenever the discount is already expressed as a percent. Comparing the old price to what you'd actually pay now is also how you'd check whether an advertised markdown is genuinely as steep as it claims.

Pay Cuts and Decrease in Expenses

The same formula applies to money moving the other way. A company that cut a department's budget from $500,000 to $400,000 in a year achieved a 20% decrease in expenses. A freelancer whose pre-tax revenue fell from $80,000 to $46,400 after taxes and a slower quarter absorbed a 42% percentage drop — the same logic applies to a salary reduction, where net salary after a pay cut is just the ending number in the same equation. Startups tracking their burn rate use this monthly, watching the difference between last month's spending and this month's to see whether costs are actually trending down.

Population Decline Rate Over Time

Demographers use a population decline rate the same way retailers use a markdown: a town that fell from 1,000 residents to 950 in a year experienced a 5% decline. Expressing a shrinking headcount as a percentage — rather than a raw number — lets you compare places of very different sizes on equal footing, since losing 50 residents means something very different for a small town than for a large city.

Absolute Decrease vs. Percentage Decrease

An absolute decrease is just the raw difference between two numbers — no division, no percentage sign. A relative decrease (the percentage version) tells you far more about the real significance of that drop. Losing $1,000,000 in profit sounds identical whether a company made $2,000,000 or $100,000,000 the year before, but as a percentage those two cases are 50% and 1% respectively — one is a crisis, the other barely a rounding error. The same idea applies to weight loss: dropping 10 pounds off a 200-pound starting weight is a 5% decrease, more useful context than the raw number alone gives you, especially against a healthy weight range where the percentage matters more than the figure by itself.

Once you can calculate percentage decrease for one kind of number, you can calculate percent decrease for any of them — the percent decrease formula above is the shared unit that makes a $20 markdown, a 5-pound weight loss, and a shrinking town's headcount directly comparable to each other. It's the same reason a percent decrease calculator and a percent reduction calculator are really the same tool wearing two different names.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q.How do I subtract 25% from a number?
Multiply the number by 0.75. For example, 200 - 25% = 200 × 0.75 = 150. The decrease amount is 50.
Q.What is 80 minus 15 percent?
80 minus 15% equals 68. Calculation: 80 × 0.15 = 12 (decrease), then 80 - 12 = 68.
Q.How do I calculate depreciation?
Multiply the asset value by (1 - depreciation rate). For 20% depreciation on $10,000: $10,000 × 0.80 = $8,000.
Q.What is the formula for subtracting a percentage?
New Value = Original × (1 - Percentage/100). For 30% off $50: $50 × 0.70 = $35.
Q.How much is 40% off $120?
$120 with 40% off = $72. Calculation: $120 × 0.60 = $72. You save $48.
Q.What does a 100% decrease mean?
A 100% decrease reduces the value to zero. 50 decreased by 100% = 50 - 50 = 0.
Q.Why do I need more than 50% gain to recover from 50% loss?
Because the gain percentage applies to the reduced amount. After 50% loss, you have half. To double back, you need 100% gain.
Q.How do consecutive discounts work?
They multiply, not add. 20% off then 10% off: 100 × 0.80 × 0.90 = 72 (28% total, not 30%).
Q.What is 500 decreased by 35%?
500 decreased by 35% = 325. Calculation: 500 × 0.65 = 325. The decrease is 175.
Q.How do I find the original price before a discount?
Divide the sale price by (1 - discount%). If you paid $60 after 25% off: $60 ÷ 0.75 = $80 original.
Q.Can a decrease be negative?
Entering a negative percentage would actually increase the value. Use the increase calculator for additions.
Q.What is 1000 minus 7.5%?
1000 minus 7.5% = 925. Calculation: 1000 × 0.075 = 75, then 1000 - 75 = 925.
