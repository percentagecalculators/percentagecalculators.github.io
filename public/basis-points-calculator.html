What Is a Basis Point (bps)?

A basis point — abbreviated bps and pronounced "bip" — is the smallest standard unit finance uses to describe a change in a percentage rate. One basis point equals 1/100th of 1%, or 0.0001 in decimal form: \( 1\ \text{bps} = 0.01\% = 0.0001 \). A hundred basis points make up a full percentage point, and ten thousand basis points make up an entire percent's worth of movement — 100%.

In the language of proportions, a basis point is the same fraction as a permyriad, 1/10,000 of a whole, while a permille is 1/1,000. Basis points, permilles, and permyriads all describe the same kind of ratio; finance simply settled on "basis point" as its everyday name for it.

Why Finance Uses Basis Points Instead of Percentages

Saying a rate "increased by 10%" is genuinely ambiguous. If your commission is usually 10% and it increased by 10%, is your new rate 11% or 20%? Both readings are mathematically valid, which is exactly the kind of ambiguity basis points were built to remove — a 100 bps increase always means the rate went up by exactly one percentage point, no interpretation required.

The same problem shows up outside finance too. If a country's unemployment rate moved from 6% to 16%, describing that jump as "a 10% increase" is confusing, since it could mean either the small relative change or the large absolute one. Saying it rose by 1,000 basis points instead removes the guesswork. That precision is why interest rates, bond yields, and other financial markets data are almost always quoted in bps rather than raw percentage change.

Basis Points, Percentage, and Decimal at a Glance

  • 1 bps = 0.01% = 0.0001
  • 10 bps = 0.10% = 0.001
  • 100 bps = 1.00% = 0.01
  • 1,000 bps = 10.00% = 0.10

Even small differences compound quickly once a principal amount is attached — which is exactly why lenders, fund managers, and traders track changes this granular in the first place.

Basis Point Conversion: Converting BPS to a Percentage

The formula behind every basis point conversion is simple division or multiplication by 100:

$$ \text{Percentage} = \frac{\text{Basis Points}}{100} $$

$$ \text{Basis Points} = \text{Percentage} \times 100 $$

If you'd rather convert by hand than let the calculator do it:

  1. Take the number of basis points you were given.
  2. Divide it by 100 to get a percentage, or multiply a percentage by 100 to get basis points.
  3. Double-check the direction — bps to percent gets smaller, percent to bps gets larger.

Basis Points to Decimal Form

To go straight from basis points to decimal form, divide by 10,000 instead of 100:

$$ \text{Decimal} = \frac{\text{Basis Points}}{10{,}000} $$

So 250 basis points converts to 2.50% or, in decimal form, 0.025 — the same figure you'd plug into most spreadsheet interest-rate formulas. Use whichever direction you need to convert.

Percentage to Basis Points Formula

Going the other direction uses the same formula flipped: multiply a percentage by 100. Take one more example: a rate that increased from 4.00% to 4.09% moved by 0.09 percentage points, which converts to 9 basis points — precisely the kind of small rate change that would be awkward to describe any other way.

Calculating the Dollar Value of BPS

Basis points only become useful once you attach them to a principal amount. The formula for the monetary value of a basis point move is:

$$ \text{Value} = \text{Principal} \times \left(\frac{\text{Basis Points}}{10{,}000}\right) $$

For a $100,000 loan balance, 50 basis points works out to $100,000 × 0.005 = $500 — the kind of number that matters a great deal more than "half a percent" makes it sound.

Worked Example: Commission on a Sale

Suppose you close a $150,000 sale value and earn a 30 bps commission. First, find 100 bps of the sale: $150,000 ÷ 100 = $1,500. Divide that by 100 again to isolate 1 basis point: $15. Multiply by your 30 bps commission rate and you've earned $450. The same logic applies when a stock position moves: a stock worth $65,000 that increases by 1.5 bps gains $9.75 in value. Basis points can also be negative — a bond that "decreased by 25 basis points" is easier to say, and just as accurate, as one that "increased by -25 basis points."

Where BPS Matter: Interest Rates, Bond Yields, and Investment Fees

Basis points show up anywhere a financial instrument's rate can move by less than a full percentage point:

  • Interest rate changes announced by a central bank
  • Bond yields and credit spreads on treasury bonds and corporate bonds
  • Mortgage rates on new and refinanced loans
  • Investment fees, including fund expense ratios

Interest Rates and Central Bank Decisions

When the Fed or another central bank adjusts its benchmark interest rate, the announcement is almost always phrased in basis points — "a 25 basis point hike" rather than "a 0.25 percentage point hike." Since interest rates move in increments this small so often, basis points keep every headline and every rate table unambiguous.

Bond Yields and Credit Spreads

Bond yields on treasury bonds and corporate bonds are quoted in basis points because even a handful of bps can shift a fixed income portfolio's value meaningfully. The same unit measures credit spreads — the gap between two bond yields. A 10 bps spread between two similar bonds is common in short-term markets, and traders watch that spread widen or narrow the way you'd watch any other rate move, alongside other debt securities, interest rate derivatives, and credit derivatives. Analysts also use basis points to describe changes across loans generally, including mortgage loans, since these financial instruments all trade on rate differences too small for whole percentages to capture cleanly.

Mortgage Rates and Investment Fees

Mortgage rates typically move in increments of an eighth of a percentage point, or 12.5 basis points, so a lender quoting "up 15 bps since last week" is being precise rather than vague. Investment fees follow the same convention: mutual funds and ETFs usually express their expense ratios and management fees in basis points, since 100 bps equals exactly 1% — a fee described as "45 bps" is instantly comparable across funds in a way "0.45%" tends not to be at a glance.

BPS vs. Percentage Points: Why the Difference Matters

A percentage point and a basis point measure the same kind of movement at different scales — one basis point is simply 1/100th of a percentage point. Mixing them up is an easy mistake with real consequences: mistaking "50 basis points" for "50 percentage points" would overstate a rate move by a factor of 100.

Percentage Change vs. Basis Point Change

The core difference is precision. A relative percentage change ("rates rose 10%") depends on the starting value, while a basis point change is absolute and small by design, which is exactly why financial markets default to it when reporting rate changes.
