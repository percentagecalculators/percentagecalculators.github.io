Whether you're checking a chemistry titration or double-checking a physics lab result, a Percentage Error Calculator gives you a fast way to see exactly how far your measured number strayed from the value it was supposed to hit. Feed it your observed and true values and it hands back the one number that actually tells you whether your experiment worked: a clean, comparable percentage instead of a raw difference that's hard to judge on its own. You'll find this kind of check useful anywhere a discrepancy between what you measured and what you expected needs to be quantified — not just in a school lab, but any time accuracy matters more than the raw numbers alone.

What Is a Percentage Error Calculator?

A percentage error calculator compares two numbers — the value you observed and the value that's actually correct — and converts the gap between them into a percentage. That gap goes by several names depending on the textbook you're using: you'll see it called relative error, approximation error, or simply experimental error, and all of them describe the same underlying idea. Instead of leaving you with an awkward raw difference like "0.3 ounces off" or "1.5°C high," percent error normalizes that difference so you can compare results across completely different units and scales.

This matters because a raw difference on its own doesn't tell you much. Being off by 2 grams sounds tiny until you realize the true value was only 3 grams — that's actually a huge miss. A percent error calculator strips out that ambiguity by expressing every measurement's discrepancy the same way, which is exactly why it shows up constantly in laboratory science, engineering coursework, and any other field built on careful data analysis. In practice, checking your percent error is just the scientific method's usual "measure, compare, and verify" loop applied to a single number.

The Percent Error Formula Explained

Every percent error calculator runs on the same formula. You take the difference between your observed value and the true value, take the absolute value of that difference, divide by the true value, and multiply by 100:

$$ \%\ \text{error} = \left| \frac{V_{\text{observed}} - V_{\text{true}}}{V_{\text{true}}} \right| \times 100 $$

In this formula, V stands for value — V_observed is what you measured, and V_true is the accepted or theoretical figure you're measuring against. Getting the numerator and denominator right is the entire game here: the numerator is always the difference between your two values, and the denominator is always the true value, never the one you measured.

Absolute Error Formula

Before you can calculate percent error, you need the absolute error — just the plain difference between your observed and true values, with the sign stripped out by the absolute value bars:

$$ \text{absolute error} = |V_{\text{observed}} - V_{\text{true}}| $$

If your scale reads 1.2 pounds but the actual weight is 1.5 pounds, your absolute error is simply 0.3 pounds — no percentages yet, just the raw gap.

Relative Error Formula

Divide that absolute error by the true value and you get the relative error formula, which is the decimal form of percent error before you multiply by 100:

$$ \text{relative error} = \left| \frac{V_{\text{observed}} - V_{\text{true}}}{V_{\text{true}}} \right| $$

A relative error of 0.05 is the same thing as a percent error of 5% — multiplying by 100 is the only step that separates the two.

Experimental Value vs. Theoretical Value: Getting the Terms Right

One of the most common sources of confusion isn't the math — it's the vocabulary. Textbooks use a dozen different words for the exact same two numbers, and mixing them up is an easy way to divide by the wrong one.

  • Theoretical value — also called the true value, accepted value, actual value, or standard value. This is the "book answer" you're comparing against.
  • Experimental value — also called the observed value, measured value, estimated value, or approximate value. This is what you actually got in the lab.

Common Names for Experimental and Theoretical Values

Role in the FormulaAlso CalledExample
Theoretical value (denominator)True value, accepted value, actual value9.80 m/s² for gravity
Experimental value (numerator, with theoretical)Observed value, measured value, estimated value9.72 m/s² you measured

Once you know which value plays which role, the formula stops being confusing — you're always subtracting, then dividing by whichever number represents the truth, regardless of what your textbook happens to call it.

How to Calculate Percent Error Step by Step

You can run the whole calculation by hand in four steps:

  1. Identify which number is the theoretical (true) value and which is your experimental (measured) value.
  2. Subtract the theoretical value from the experimental value to find the difference between the two.
  3. Take the absolute value of that result, then divide by the theoretical value.
  4. Multiply by 100 to convert your relative error into a percentage.

Worked Example: Chemistry Lab (Specific Heat)

Say you're running a calorimetry experiment to find the specific heat of aluminum. The accepted value is 0.903 J/g°C, and your measured result came out to 0.880 J/g°C. Subtracting gives you a difference of -0.023; taking the absolute value gives 0.023. Divide that by the true value of 0.903 and you get roughly 0.0255, or 2.55% error once you multiply by 100 — a strong result for a lab report, well under the 5% threshold most instructors expect.

Worked Example: Physics Lab (Gravity)

Now suppose you're timing a pendulum's swing to estimate the acceleration due to gravity, and free fall isn't quite ideal in your setup. The accepted gravitational acceleration is 9.81 m/s², but your result comes out to 10.5 m/s². The difference is 0.69, and dividing by 9.81 gives about 0.0703 — a 7.03% error, which is noticeably higher and would point you toward checking for air resistance or a timing mistake before writing up your results.

Negative Percentage Error: What a Negative Sign Means

By default, percent error is always reported as a positive number because of the absolute value bars in the formula — most of the time, only the size of the discrepancy matters, not its direction. But if you skip the absolute value step and allow a signed percent, you can get a negative percentage error. That simply means your observed value came in lower than the true value; a positive signed result means it came in higher. Neither direction is "better" — a percent error of exactly 0 is the only outcome that means your observed and true values matched perfectly.

Quick tip — the denominator trap: mixing up which value goes on the bottom of the fraction is one of the most common lab technique mistakes students make. Always divide by the accepted, theoretical figure — never the one you measured yourself.

Percent Error vs. Percent Difference vs. Standard Error

Percent error isn't the only tool for comparing numbers, and mixing it up with its close relatives is a fast way to misreport your results.

When There's No True Value: Percent Difference

Use percent difference instead of percent error whenever you're comparing two measured values and neither one is a known, accepted figure. Instead of dividing by a true value, you divide by the average of the two:

$$ \text{percent difference} = \frac{|A - B|}{\left(\frac{|A| + |B|}{2}\right)} \times 100 $$

For example, if two trials measuring the speed of sound gave you 329 m/s and 343 m/s, you'd take the difference between them (14), divide by the average of the two (336), and multiply by 100 — a percent difference calculation, not a percent error one, since neither reading is the "true" reference value.

Standard Error and Margin of Error

When you're working with a whole data set rather than a single measurement, standard deviation and standard error take over from percent error as your accuracy check. Standard error describes how precisely a sample's average represents the full population, and it feeds directly into the margin of error you'd report alongside a confidence interval — multiply standard error by the appropriate z-score and you get the margin of error for a given confidence level.

What Counts as a Good Percent Error in Chemistry and Physics?

"Good" is relative to the field, the equipment, and how many significant figures your measurement can realistically support — it usually comes down to a mix of accuracy and precision rather than a single fixed cutoff. As a general guide:

ContextTypically Acceptable Percent Error
High school science labsUnder 10%
General chemistry and physics experimentsUnder 5%
Analytical chemistryUnder 0.1%–0.01%
Biological fieldwork10%–20% due to natural variance

An error over 100% almost always signals a real mistake — a unit conversion slip, a broken piece of equipment, or a fundamentally flawed experiment — rather than ordinary human error or measurement noise. Whatever field you're in, treating percent error, relative error, and significant figures together gives you a much fuller picture of accuracy than any single number in isolation, and it's the same logic that makes percent error such a standard tool across science, statistics, and everyday word problems involving a difference between two values.

Next time you finish an experiment, run your numbers through a percent error calculator before you write anything down — it takes seconds, and it's the difference between catching a mistake early and finding out about it after your lab report is already graded.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q.What is the percent error formula for chemistry?
Same as physics: (|Experimental - Accepted| / Accepted) × 100. It's universal across sciences.
Q.What is a good percentage error for a physics lab?
Generally <5% is good. <1% is excellent. Acceptable levels depend on equipment and experiment type.
Q.Is percentage error always positive?
Standard percent error uses absolute value, so it's always positive (or zero for perfect accuracy).
Q.What causes high percentage error in experiments?
Common causes: equipment calibration, measurement technique, environmental factors, calculation mistakes, procedural errors.
Q.Can percentage error be zero?
Yes, if your experimental value exactly equals the theoretical value.
Q.What is the difference between accuracy and precision?
Accuracy = how close to true value (percent error). Precision = how consistent repeated measurements are (standard deviation).
Q.How do I reduce percentage error?
Use calibrated instruments, take multiple measurements and average, improve technique, control variables.
Q.What if the theoretical value is zero?
Percent error is undefined (division by zero). Use absolute error instead.
Q.How do I report error in a lab report?
State: 'The percentage error was X%' and include likely sources of error (equipment, procedure, environment).
Q.What is relative error vs percent error?
Relative error = |Exp - Theo| / |Theo|. Percent error = relative error × 100.
Q.Is 10% error acceptable?
Depends on context. For rough estimates, yes. For precision instruments, no. Compare to expected accuracy of your equipment.
Q.How do I calculate error for multiple trials?
Calculate percent error for each trial, or use the average of trials as your experimental value, then calculate single percent error.
